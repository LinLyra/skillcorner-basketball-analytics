# Beyond the Snapshot

## Evaluating the reliability and incremental value of temporal defensive context in professional basketball

![Revised evidence summary](figures/revision_summary.png)

## Abstract

Basketball shot-quality analyses often represent defense using the nearest defender at release. Tracking data makes richer temporal descriptions possible, but additional motion variables may be noisy, unstable across games, or redundant with mature production models. This study uses the ten complete 25 Hz professional games in SkillCorner Open Data Basketball to test whether 0.5-, 1-, and 2-second pre-release defensive histories improve out-of-game shot-outcome probabilities beyond location, shot context, and release distance. It audits event alignment, compares defender definitions, propagates vendor position-error bounds, uses exact small-cluster inference and conditional negative controls, tests nested tuned linear and monotonic nonlinear models, and treats SkillCorner Shot Quality as an external reference rather than a like-for-like competitor.

Among 1,459 shots, 1,433 had usable shooter–defender trajectories and 1,211 non-fouled shots met the primary event/tracking alignment rule. A one-second temporal model improved mean leave-one-game-out log loss from 0.6646 to 0.6633, but its paired game-cluster interval crossed zero (difference −0.00125, 95% bootstrap interval [−0.00586, 0.00316]); the exact two-sided sign-flip p-value was 0.613. Inverting that exact test gives a 95% compatible location interval of [−0.00690, 0.00428]. After cross-fitted removal of information predictable from static/context features, 15.5% of within-game residual permutations performed at least as well as the observed data. Thus the earlier unconditional-permutation result does not survive a fairer conditional control. At a retrospective practical reference of 0.005 log-loss units, the study is inconclusive: it cannot rule out an improvement as large as roughly 0.0069. Alternative defender definitions, quality filters, smoothing, subgroups, nested tuning, and a monotonic tree likewise do not resolve the small observed increment from zero. Defender-policy sensitivity and position-error propagation are reported without causal interpretation.

The main contribution is therefore a reproducible boundary and an audit protocol: this sample does not support a stable incremental-value claim for simple temporal separation summaries. Defender identity, event alignment, position uncertainty, model class, and benchmark provenance materially change the interpretation.

## Research questions

1. Does temporal defensive context improve shot-outcome probability estimates beyond a release-time snapshot?
2. Which history window—0.5, 1, or 2 seconds—generalizes best to unseen games?
3. Are results robust to defender definition, tracking quality, trajectory smoothing, and basketball context?
4. Does temporal information add value beyond SkillCorner’s official Shot Quality benchmark?

## Data and cohort construction

The source is SkillCorner Open Data Basketball at the commit recorded in `datasets.lock.json`. Tracking is sampled at 25 Hz. Dynamic Events provide shot frames, shooter and defender identities, outcomes, context fields, and official Shot Quality.

Data audit:

- 10 professional games;
- 1,459 shot events;
- 1,433 shots with a usable shooter–defender release trajectory;
- 26 shots missing the official release defender in tracking;
- 80 shots with an official/recomputed release-distance discrepancy above 0.05 m;
- 1,211 non-fouled, aligned shots in the primary cohort;
- 44.9% made-shot rate.

The 0.05 m rule is an alignment check, not a claim about the absolute accuracy of broadcast tracking. `predError` is a vendor-provided 90% error radius, not a Gaussian standard deviation. For sensitivity analysis only, it is converted to an axis standard deviation under an explicit isotropic-Gaussian assumption: `sigma = predError / sqrt(-2 log(0.1))`.

## Models

- **M0 — location/context:** shot distance, shot clock, two/three-point status, catch-and-shoot, prior dribbles, region, and shot type.
- **M1 — static defense:** M0 plus defender distance at release.
- **M2 — temporal defense:** M1 plus recent closing speed, separation gain, pressure duration, and distance summaries.
- **M3 — multiplayer geometry:** M2 plus second/third defender distances, defensive density, defender-to-hoop angle, shooting-lane obstruction, teammate spacing, help-defender closing, and density change.
- **M4 — official recalibrated:** a logistic recalibration of official SkillCorner Shot Quality.
- **M5 — official plus temporal:** official Shot Quality plus the M2 temporal summaries.

M0–M5 use regularized logistic regression and fold-local preprocessing. The goal is controlled comparison and probability estimation, not architecture novelty.

## Validation

Every learned prediction is generated by leave-one-game-out validation. Preprocessing is fitted only on the nine training games. Primary metrics are log loss and Brier score, with AUC and calibration as secondary diagnostics. The primary descriptive interval uses 20,000 paired game-cluster bootstrap resamples; because there are only ten clusters, it is supplemented by all 2^10 exact sign flips. Conditional permutation first predicts each temporal feature from static/context variables in cross-fitting, then permutes the residual vector within game. Model-class sensitivity uses nested LOGO tuning and a small monotonic histogram-gradient tree.

## Results

### Static and temporal comparison

| Model | Mean log loss | Mean Brier | Mean AUC |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| M0 location/context | 0.6677 | 0.2369 | 0.6216 |
| M1 static defense | 0.6646 | 0.2356 | 0.6310 |
| M2 temporal, 0.5 s | 0.6647 | 0.2356 | 0.6290 |
| M2 temporal, 1.0 s | **0.6633** | **0.2350** | **0.6333** |
| M2 temporal, 2.0 s | 0.6666 | 0.2364 | 0.6291 |

One second is the strongest tested temporal window. Two seconds is worse than the static model, consistent with older history adding assignment changes, irrelevant movement, or noise.

The out-of-game training-rate baseline has mean log loss 0.6899. Relative to it, M0 improves by 0.0222, M1 by 0.0253, and M2 by 0.0266. The nested monotonic static tree reaches 0.6487, an improvement of 0.0413. The external official score improves by 0.0798 versus the same reference—about 1.9 times the tree improvement—but the provenance caveat below prevents treating this as a fair modeling contest.

The one-second improvement over M1 is small and heterogeneous: Δ log loss −0.00125, 95% interval [−0.00586, 0.00316], with improvement in six of ten games.

### Robustness

None of six defender-definition/tracking-quality combinations excludes zero. Five-frame centered-median smoothing produces Δ log loss −0.00114 [−0.00563, 0.00314], almost identical to the raw result.

Under frame-wise nearest-defender tracking, defender identity changes during the one-second history for 525 of 1,458 valid trajectories (36.0%). This quantifies a known definitional property; it is not presented as a newly discovered practitioner blind spot. A backward, release-anchored hysteresis policy that requires a challenger to be at least 0.3 m closer reduces the switch rate to 436 of 1,433 valid trajectories (30.4%).

Across 1,209 common out-of-game predictions, fixed and frame-nearest policies correlate at 0.988, yet their mean absolute difference is 0.0141 and the 95th percentile is 0.0522; 68 shots differ by more than five percentage points. When identity switches, mean absolute difference is 0.0256, compared with 0.0075 without a switch. This association is descriptive: switching occurs in crowded, ambiguous possessions and is not randomized, so it cannot be interpreted causally.

On the identical 1,209-shot policy-comparison cohort, mean log loss is 0.66450 for fixed release defender, 0.66472 for frame-nearest, and 0.66453 for 0.3 m hysteresis. Relative to fixed, frame-nearest changes log loss by +0.00022 [−0.00304, 0.00308], while hysteresis changes it by +0.00002 [−0.00327, 0.00287]. No policy is detectably more accurate. The value of the analysis is therefore transparency and construct definition—not a claim that switching improves or harms prediction.

### Small-cluster inference and conditional negative control

The exact two-sided sign-flip p-value for the ten game-level differences is 0.613 (one-sided 0.307), with inverted 95% compatible location interval [−0.00690, 0.00428]. A naive whole-vector permutation produced an apparently strong result, but it also destroyed dependence between temporal variables and release distance. It is retained only as a diagnostic. Under the stricter cross-fitted residual permutation, the null mean is +0.00052, the 95% range is [−0.00411, 0.00376], and 15.5% of permutations are at least as favorable as the observed −0.00125. The design therefore cannot distinguish the small point estimate from zero and cannot rule out an operationally relevant improvement near 0.006–0.007.

### Model-class sensitivity

Nested LOGO tuning gives log loss 0.6631 for static and 0.6620 for temporal logistic regression (difference −0.00119 [−0.00336, 0.00123]). A monotonic nonlinear tree improves absolute fit (0.6487 static) but becomes slightly worse with temporal variables (0.6495; difference +0.00081 [−0.00295, 0.00379]). The inconclusive incremental result is therefore not specific to the original fixed regularization or linear-additive form.

### Position-error propagation

Two hundred Monte Carlo reconstructions perturb shooter and defender x/y coordinates independently under the documented isotropic-Gaussian interpretation of the vendor's 90% radial `predError`. The primary 1,211-shot cohort is held fixed so the experiment measures feature and prediction sensitivity rather than selection changes. Across draws, mean M2−M1 log-loss difference is −0.00105; the Monte Carlo 2.5th–97.5th percentile range is [−0.00382, 0.00115], and 154/200 draws favor M2. Position uncertainty does not qualitatively resolve the inference: the incremental effect remains small and the reconstruction range crosses zero. This range describes sensitivity to the assumed measurement-error model, not a confidence interval for the population effect.

### Basketball contexts

Catch-and-shoot, pull-up, two-point, three-point, official-closeout, and closing-speed subgroup intervals all cross zero. These exploratory comparisons provide no defensible subgroup claim.

### Multiplayer geometry

M3 produces mean log loss 0.6651, worse than both M1 and M2. The added feature block does not generalize with the current cohort and fixed regularization. This is evidence against indiscriminate feature expansion, not evidence that help defense is irrelevant.

### Official benchmark

| Model | Mean log loss | Mean Brier | Mean AUC |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| M2 temporal defense | 0.6633 | 0.2350 | 0.6333 |
| Official Shot Quality | **0.6101** | **0.2123** | **0.7117** |
| M4 official recalibrated | 0.6129 | 0.2127 | 0.7117 |
| M5 official plus temporal | 0.6147 | 0.2135 | 0.7062 |

Official Shot Quality is numerically stronger than the open reconstruction in all ten games, with Δ log loss +0.0532 [0.0365, 0.0697]. This is not a fair model-development contest: its training data, feature set, and possible overlap with these ten games are unknown and may be much larger. It is therefore an external production reference. Within this sample, adding the simple temporal block to a recalibration of that score does not produce a detectable improvement; this is not evidence that the dynamic information has zero value.

## Interpretation

The study supports three conclusions:

1. Release-time defender distance adds useful information to a basic open model.
2. The observed temporal gain is small, heterogeneous, non-significant under exact sign flips, and not exceptional under conditional permutation.
3. A richer external Shot Quality score is numerically much stronger, but unknown training provenance prevents a like-for-like inference; adding many geometric variables without sufficient sample size or structure can reduce generalization.

## Limitations

- Only ten complete tracking games are public.
- Outcomes are binary and intrinsically noisy; individual shooter effects cannot be estimated reliably.
- Tracking is derived from broadcast video and contains extrapolated positions.
- The `predError` sensitivity model assumes independent isotropic Gaussian errors across players and frames; real tracking errors may be directional and temporally correlated.
- Official Shot Quality is a strong but not fully reconstructable benchmark, so this study evaluates it rather than claiming a like-for-like replication.
- Orientation, limb position, contest height, screen navigation, and tactical intent are unavailable.
- Subgroup analyses are exploratory and underpowered.

## Reproducibility

Source and dataset versions are pinned. Raw data is excluded from Git. Every derived result can be rebuilt with the commands in `README.md`; automated tests cover normalization, alignment, temporal features, defender switching, smoothing invariance, spatial geometry, cross-game prediction, and bootstrap direction.

## Conclusion

The practical lesson is not that temporal defense is useless. It is that tracking-derived complexity must earn its place through out-of-game validation, uncertainty analysis, and comparison with a strong external benchmark. In this dataset, the defensible contribution is a transparent boundary result and a reusable evaluation protocol—not a new universal pressure score.
